Sending money to India: how to compare transfer services

Updated November 2026 · 3 min read

India receives more remittances than any other country. The cost of sending them varies widely, and most of it is hidden inside the exchange rate.

Two costs, one of them hidden

Every transfer has a visible fee and an exchange-rate margin. The margin is the gap between the mid-market rate, shown on our currency page, and the rate the service gives you. A service that advertises “zero fees” can still be the most expensive if its rate is poor.

Suppose the mid-market rate is ₹88.00 to a dollar and you send $1,000.

ServiceFeeRate offeredRupees received
A: “zero fee”$0₹86.20₹86,200
B: low fee$5₹87.80₹87,361

Service B charges a fee and still delivers ₹1,161 more. The only fair comparison is the last column: rupees received for the same amount sent, at the same moment.

Other things to compare

  • Speed. From minutes to three working days. Faster is sometimes dearer.
  • Funding method. Bank transfer is usually cheapest; card funding costs more.
  • Rate lock. Some services fix the rate when you book, others when funds arrive.
  • Limits and tiers. Margins often shrink for larger amounts.
  • Regulation. Use a service licensed where you live and check how complaints are handled.

Which Indian account should receive it?

Non-resident Indians typically hold two kinds of rupee account.

  • NRE account: for money earned abroad. The balance and interest can be freely taken back overseas, and interest is tax-free in India while you remain non-resident.
  • NRO account: for income arising in India, such as rent, dividends or pension. Interest is taxable in India and tax is deducted at source; taking money abroad is allowed within limits and with paperwork.

Money sent directly to a family member’s ordinary savings account is theirs to spend. Gifts received from specified close relatives are not taxed as the recipient’s income in India.

Tax on the sending side

Some countries tax or report outbound transfers. In the United States, a 1% tax applies from 2026 to remittances funded with cash, money orders or cashier’s cheques, while transfers funded from a bank account or card fall outside it. Rules like this change, so check the current position where you live.

Timing the rate

Nobody predicts exchange rates reliably. If you send regularly, sending a fixed amount on a fixed date averages out the swings. For a large one-off transfer, splitting it into two or three parts reduces the risk of converting everything on a bad day. A rate alert helps you act when the rupee is weaker, which is when a sender gets more rupees.
